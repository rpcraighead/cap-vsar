THE FLIGHT PLANNER

The first app became the Air Operations desk.

Before there was a simulator project there was a flight planner: a route and fuel planner for a squadron's Cessna 172S, written with an AI coding assistant. It was folded into CAP-VSAR in September 2026, re-based on CAP 487, and given the one job the exercise needed most: turning an assignment into a search pattern the crew can fly.

The planner with a search pattern

WHERE IT CAME FROM

A route planner first, a search-pattern tool now.

It started as a stand-alone web app. Its own first commit, in February 2026, says it was built with an AI assistant, and its README says in under four hours. It planned routes for a Cessna 172S with real performance tables, charts, terrain, weather and weight and balance, and it ran as a public web service on a cloud platform. In July it got a map-first screen and a built-in guide.

The exercise needed something it did not have: a way to build the Air Operations Branch Director's assignment as flyable waypoints. In September 2026 the planner was brought into the CAP-VSAR project. The original stand-alone line still exists in its own repository; the copy inside this project is the one in use.

WHAT IT DOES NOW

Plan the flight, then build the search.

  • Routes. Type airports, navaids and your own points, or drag a leg's midpoint on the map to bend it and snap to a nearby fix. The navigation log gives distance, true course, wind correction, magnetic heading (from the World Magnetic Model), ground speed, time and fuel, with a fuel breakdown for taxi and takeoff, climb, cruise and reserve.
  • The airplane. Cruise, takeoff and landing figures come from a Cessna 182T information manual sample for CAP 487, and weight and balance checks the loaded airplane against its weight-dependent center-of-gravity envelope.
  • Charts. The FAA sectional and the IFR low and high charts, served from a tile cache on the box, so a base with no internet still has a map over any area that was cached while online.
  • Terrain, airports and weather. A terrain profile with a clearance rating, airport and runway details, and weather from outside services when a connection exists. With none, weather is marked simulated and the route says it was planned in still air.
  • Search patterns. Expanding square (SS), creeping line (CS) and parallel track (PS), built from a datum or a box and named the way the G1000's search pages name them: DATUM, SS01 and so on. Apply to Route puts them in the navigation log.
  • Exports. Export .FMS writes an X-Plane flight plan for the G1000, up to 97 pattern waypoints. Push to V-SAR creates the sortie's scenario in the exercise console, with the search area's center as the alert and a 5 nautical mile alert radius, and broadcasts a message. The pattern's waypoints stay in the planner and in the .fms file; the console receives the center and the count. Pushing needs a signed-in Exercise Director; building a pattern and downloading the file need no sign-in.

The planner and the G1000's search pages share their pattern geometry and are tested against one fixture, so the pattern in the navigation log and the one on the display agree to a few meters.

THE SEARCH PATTERN DIALOG

From an assignment to a flyable pattern.

The Air Operations Branch Director takes the assignment from the ICS 204 and opens the pattern dialog. The datum can be a fix, an airport or a point typed as latitude and longitude. The dialog asks for the track spacing and legs for an expanding square, for the start and end and the sweep width of a creeping line, or for the box and the lane spacing of a parallel track, and previews the result on the map with its length, time and fuel.

The example here is the exercise's own datum, the ESCON fix on the approach to KCRQ, with a one-mile spacing and eight legs: nine waypoints, about twenty nautical miles.

When the pattern is pushed to the console, the practice beacon is not placed on the datum. The planner moves it a fixed distance, 2.5 nautical miles by default, on a bearing worked out from the plan, so the same plan always gives the same beacon and the plan does not hand the crew the answer.

WHO USES IT

Mostly the Air Operations desk, before launch.

Who uses the Flight Planner and for what
SeatWhat they do in it
Air Operations Branch DirectorBuilds the patterns from the ICS 204 assignments and exports the .fms file the crew loads into the G1000.
Exercise DirectorPushes a planned sortie to the console, which creates the scenario with the search area as its alert and tells Mission Base.
Mission Pilot and Mission ObserverThe exercise guide lists them with the Air Operations Branch Director for this step: build or check the pattern, read the terrain profile, load the file in the simulator and fly the assignment the base is tracking.

It runs on the same box as the rest of the exercise, as a container reached under /planner/ on the same address, so one sign-in covers the portal, Mission Base and the planner. How that box is laid out is on the Hosting page.

THE AIRPLANE'S NUMBERS

A baseline, stated as one.

CAP 487's figures come from the 182T's information manual: limits, takeoff and landing tables, climb and cruise. Weight and balance starts from the manual's sample empty weight and moment, 1,924 pounds and 70.9 thousand pound-inches, which is a baseline for planning and not a measurement of the simulated airplane. The aircraft builder uses 1,970 pounds, and the planner's own screen says that the simulator's setting must be changed to match before the two are compared.

In the example here a pilot, a front passenger, a little baggage and 60 gallons of fuel come to 2,674 pounds, inside the weight and balance limits.

Not for real navigation.

The planner is for simulation and carries a "simulation use only" notice at every start. Its airport and navaid data comes from the public OurAirports dataset, not from the FAA's own data. Terrain heights are sampled and approximate, parts of the weather can be simulated, notices to air missions are placeholders, and a few waypoint types (named RNAV intersections) are not in its data, so you drop a user waypoint at the coordinates instead. The 182T figures are a manual's sample baseline, not measurements of this airplane. Its flight-plan file follows X-Plane's published format, but the planner's own guide says loading it on the G1000 in a running simulator had not been checked, so compare the names and coordinates with the brief.
