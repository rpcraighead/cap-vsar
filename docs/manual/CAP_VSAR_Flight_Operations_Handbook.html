1. System Overview & Operational Architecture

The Civil Air Patrol Virtual Search & Rescue (CAP V-SAR) training simulator provides end-to-end, procedurally authentic training for CAP aircrews, Mission Observers (MO), Mission Radio Operators (MRO), and Incident Command staff. The architecture separates simulation physics from instrument display and exercise coordination.

1.1 Distributed Multi-Station Architecture

Unlike single-seat flight simulators where one user controls all aircraft systems, CAP V-SAR implements a true crew-resource-management (CRM) environment partitioned across distinct functional layers:

  • Flight Deck / Simulator (X-Plane 12): Runs aircraft aerodynamics and the custom PI_capvsar_df.py Python plugin, probing real terrain for line-of-sight propagation, cone of confusion, and 406 MHz burst cadences.
  • Mission Observer (MO) Station: Runs on a dedicated secondary monitor or laptop via Air Manager / Air Player, presenting the G1000 MFD, GMA 1347 Audio Panel, Direction Finder head, and Technisonic TDFM-136B radio.
  • Mission Planning Module (Flight Planner): Runs as a containerized service on ms01:8090, generating geodetic search patterns and pushing flight plans to the simulator and exercise server.
  • Exercise Control & Mission Base (Web Console): Hosted on ms01:8700, providing real-time radar positions, scenario fault injection, message logging, and debrief track analysis.
Operational Note — Voice Communications Inter-crew and mission base communications are routed via an authentic radio emulation layer backed by a dedicated Mumble server (port 64738 on ms01). The MO transmits on CAP FM frequencies through the TDFM-136B and GMA 1347 COM3 channel.

2.1 CAP Search Pattern Generators

Clicking the SAR button on the main navigation toolbar launches the geodetic pattern generation tool. Three standardized CAP search patterns are supported:

Expanding Square (SS)

Used when searching for an Emergency Locator Transmitter (ELT) or missing aircraft with a known Last Known Position (LKP) or distress alert datum. The pattern expands outward in concentric squares:

  • Leg Lengths: Successive legs follow the progression 1×S, 1×S, 2×S, 2×S, 3×S, 3×S... where S is track spacing.
  • Track Spacing: Configurable between 0.5 NM and 2.0 NM depending on terrain, visibility, and target size.
  • Turns: Selectable Right (standard) or Left turns at precise 90° cardinal angles.

Creeping Line (CS)

Used to sweep an elongated search corridor, highway, valley floor, or river drainage where the wreckage is suspected along a route axis:

  • Flies cross-track sweeps perpendicular to the advance axis.
  • Sweeps alternate left and right across the track corridor (default width 3.0 NM).
  • Advances along the route by track spacing S after each cross sweep.

Parallel Track (PS)

Used for large-area systematic searches across CAP quadrangles. The aircraft sweeps parallel tracks back and forth across a rectangular search box defined by corner coordinates, axis heading, length, and width.

2.2 Simulator & Console Export Bridges

Once generated, the search pattern provides two immediate export capabilities:

  1. Export X-Plane 12 (.fms): Generates a standard FMS 1100 format flight plan containing departure, enroute fixes, and all generated search pattern waypoints (e.g. SS01, SS02...). The pilot can load this directly into the G1000 MFD flight plan catalog.
  2. Push to V-SAR Console: Transmits the planned search corridor over LAN to the exercise console (http://exercise:8700). The server creates a live scenario entry and, if an exercise sortie is active, broadcasts a tasking message to Mission Base.
Flight Tip — Fuel Reserves & Ops Normal The nav-log drawer computes true airspeed, ground speed, and fuel burn for the search pattern based on current GFS winds aloft. Always ensure estimated fuel allows for the mandatory CAP 1-hour daytime VFR reserve before committing to deep valley creeping-line searches.

3.2 The Critical GMA 1347 Audio Panel Trap

The Civil Air Patrol Mission Observer Guide emphasizes that nearly every reported in-flight radio failure is actually an audio panel configuration error. The GMA 1347 enforces strict separation between receiver audio and transmitter microphone selection:

  • Listening to COM3: Pressing the COM3 audio key lights the annunciator and routes VHF-FM radio audio to your headset. This does NOT allow you to transmit on COM3!
  • Transmitting on COM3: You must press the COM3 MIC button on the top row. The illuminated microphone button dictates which transmitter receives your PTT signal.
Critical Cockpit Trap — Audio vs. Mic Selection Selecting COM3 audio enables you to hear mission traffic, but keying the flight yoke or hand mic will transmit on COM1 (VHF Airband / ATC) if the COM1 MIC button remains selected. Always verify both the COM3 Audio and COM3 MIC annunciators are illuminated before making tactical transmissions.

3.3 Direction Finder: Becker SAR-DF 517 vs. RHOTHETA RT-600

A primary training objective of the CAP V-SAR simulator is demonstrating the operational coverage difference between the legacy Becker 517 and the modern fleet-standard RHOTHETA RT-600:

Figure 3: Dedicated C182T MO Station — Layout 2 (RHOTHETA RT-600 & TDFM-136B)
  • Becker SAR-DF 517 (Emergency Mode): The 406 COSPAS/SARSAT receiver is locked to a single fixed frequency: 406.025 MHz. If a modern beacon transmits on 406.037 MHz, the Becker in Emergency mode receives zero signal.
  • RHOTHETA RT-600 (CPSAR Groups): The RT-600 receiver features seven broad-band CPSAR groups (A through S) covering 19 distinct 3 kHz channels. When set to CPSAR2, the RT-600 detects beacons across 406.031, 406.034, and 406.037 MHz simultaneously without retuning.
  • Becker Training Mode: When switched to Training Mode, the Becker becomes freely tunable across 400–410 MHz, allowing the observer to manually tune to 406.037 MHz to re-acquire signal.

4. Instructor & Exercise Control Console

The Instructor Console (hosted on http://ms01:8700/) gives training officers comprehensive control over simulated flight exercises, real-time aircraft tracking, and dynamic environmental failure injection.

Figure 4: Instructor Control Console with Sectional Basemap & Dynamic Failure Controls

4.1 Scenario Selection & Interactive Creation

Instructors can activate pre-scripted training sorties (e.g., Palomar ILS ESCON ELT) or construct custom scenarios using the built-in New Scenario panel:

  1. Click the New scenario accordion.
  2. Click Pick on map and click anywhere on the FAA VFR Sectional chart to drop the beacon pin.
  3. Drag the amber pin to fine-tune placement (e.g. into a narrow box canyon or behind a ridge).
  4. Specify transmitter power (watts), ground elevation (ft MSL), and transmission band (121.5 / 243 / 406 MHz).
  5. Click Create scenario — the new sortie is immediately registered and ready to fly.

4.2 Injected Propagation Effects

To build realistic electronic search skills, instructors can toggle discrete radio propagation effects during flight:

  • Radio Horizon (Line-of-Sight): Computes spherical earth curvature and terrain shielding. The DF receiver remains silent until the crew climbs above the visual radio horizon.
  • 406 Burst Cadence & Timehold: Emulates 406 MHz beacon behavior — a 440 ms burst every 50 seconds. The DF head holds the decoded bearing during the silence.
  • Cone of Confusion: When the aircraft flies directly over the beacon, the relative bearing needle spins and the signal drops momentarily, confirming station passage.
  • Multipath Reflections: Injects mountain-ridge reflections that deflect the apparent bearing by up to 40°, testing the crew's ability to cross-check bearings.

5. Mission Base & Debrief Modules

5.1 Mission Base Operator View

The Mission Base console (http://ms01:8700/base) represents the situational map for the Mission Radio Operator (MRO) and Air Operations Branch Director (AOBD). In accordance with CAP training standards, beacon truth is hidden on this view.

Figure 5: Mission Base Console with Aircraft Radar Track and Tactical Message Log

As the aircrew radios in "Ops Normal" reports and bearing lines, Mission Base operators log the cuts into the console. The system projects colored bearing rays across the sectional map, allowing the staff to visualize the intersection box and direct ground search teams.

5.2 Post-Flight Debrief Replay

After the sortie is terminated, the instructor and aircrew open the Debrief Console (http://ms01:8700/debrief). The debrief engine replays the recorded flight second-by-second against the unmasked beacon truth:

Figure 6: Debrief Console with Flight Track Scoring & Bearing Error Analysis
  • Closest Point of Approach (CPA): Exact distance in nautical miles and timestamp of closest approach.
  • Track Efficiency: Compares total miles flown against direct-line distance to evaluate pattern discipline.
  • Bearing Cut Accuracy: Displays reported cockpit bearings against truth vectors to demonstrate multi-path errors and averaging lag.

5.3 Mission Radio Operator (MRO) Base Station Radio Console

De-coupled from the situational radar map, the Base Radio Console (http://ms01:8700/radio) provides a standalone, touch- and mouse-operable communications station for Mission Radio Operators using tablets (e.g. iPad) or laptops. The current console provides two selectable VHF-FM control-head presentations over one authenticated Mumble mission-net session:

Motorola APX 4500 O2 Control Head

The APX-style faceplate provides touch targets, keyboard PTT, mission-net selection, headset volume and visible connection, receive and transmit state. Net names come from the exercise radio service rather than a hard-coded frequency card.

  • Voice: Live browser audio uses the shared Mumble bridge and one client per browser station.
  • Controls: Momentary or latched PTT, net stepping, home-net selection and headset volume.
  • Logging: Transmit and receive events feed the editable CAP Form 110 log with UTC timestamps.
Figure 7: Current Mission Base Radio Console with the APX 4500 O2 presentation selected

EFJohnson 5300 Control Head

The EFJohnson presentation uses the same live voice connection, selected mission net and Form 110 log. Switching faceplates does not create a second Mumble client or interrupt the active session.

  • Shared session: APX and EFJohnson presentations are two views of the same operator connection.
  • Known gaps: Airband monitoring, operational sidetone and full hardware-key behavior remain open tracker items.

CAP Form 110 Communications Log

The radio page includes a live CAP Form 110 Communications Log. It records keyed and received events with UTC timestamps and network identity; the MRO enters the traffic text and can export the completed log as plain text.

7. Training Progression: Rehearsing a SAREX Before Flying It

A Search and Rescue Exercise (SAREX) is Civil Air Patrol's structured training event: a mission base under an Incident Commander (IC) tasks aircrews and ground teams against a scripted incident. CAP V-SAR is designed so that the SAREX is the second time a crew flies its missions, not the first. The exercise director writes the incident once; the wing runs it three times on the simulator — two weeks out, one week out and two days out — and then flies it for real. The live exercise reinforces skills learned at home instead of introducing them.

7.1 Writing the Incident Once

The scripted incident is built the way a real IC builds ICS 204 assignments. Example: a rented C182 is overdue on a VFR flight from Carlsbad (KCRQ) to Thermal (KTRM); its registered 406 MHz ELT has produced a COSPAS-SARSAT composite position in the mountains east of Julian. Three air tasks fall out of that:

Sortie Task Pattern (Handbook §2.1) Purpose
A Route search Creeping line along the intended route, 1 nm spacing Visual search for the aircraft along its planned track
B ELT search Transit to the composite; home on the 406 burst and 121.5 signal; expanding square from station passage A position good enough to hand to a ground team
C Grid search Parallel track of a 15-minute grid quadrant, 1 nm spacing Cover the case where the ELT position is wrong

The instructor creates the scenario in the Exercise Control Console (§4): beacon on a ridge, 406.025 MHz training frequency, and an initial effect set of radio horizon and terrain masking only. Beacon truth is withheld from every other view until debrief.

7.2 The Three Sessions

Session What happens What is learned
1 — T-14 days
Mission base stands up		 Nothing is flown. The AOBD builds the three patterns in the Flight Planner from the ICS 204s, exports each as an X-Plane .fms and pushes it to the base map; the terrain profile catches a leg with poor clearance. The MRO joins the mission net and works the channel plan. The MOs drill the MO station: GMA 1347 COM3 MIC selection (§3.2), TDFM-136B channel entry, DF head power-up, squelch, scan, and tuning 406.025 not 406.037. Radio checks and the first 30-minute ops-normal cycle. Every seat's equipment and the base's communications rhythm are familiar before the mission starts.
2 — T-7 days
Full mission		 The IC briefs on Mumble. Sortie A flies the creeping line and calls each leg; Sortie C flies the grid and meets wing blanking in a turn. Sortie B homes on the 406 burst; the instructor injects cone of confusion as the aircraft nears the beacon, then multipath. The MO takes one bad bearing; the AOBD's plotted cut disagrees with the previous three and the base asks the crew to re-fly the leg. The IC retasks Sortie A onto a second grid through the MRO. Debrief reveals the beacon and scores every bearing against truth. Recognising station passage and multipath; base staff cross-checking the cockpit; retasking and ops-normal discipline with three aircraft airborne. The SET notes which SQTR tasks were demonstrated.
3 — T-2 days
Dress rehearsal		 Everything is now exactly as it will be on the live day: real callsigns, the real communications plan, the real grid numbers and timing, the same base staff in the same roles, and the composite position moved to where the practice beacon will actually be. Crews fly it cold; the instructor injects the effects that gave trouble in Session 2. Confirmation. Sortie B finds the beacon on the first expanding square; the bearing plot has no rejected cuts; ops-normal reports are on time for all three aircraft.
Live SAREX The same three assignments against a real practice beacon on 121.775 MHz, with real terrain, weather, radios and a ground team. Every procedure has been performed at least twice; the live day adds the airplane, the weather and the pressure. The SET signs off tasks demonstrated on both the rehearsal and the live sortie.
